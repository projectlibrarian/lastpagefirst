Accidental Project Managers

The Formula

Project Success = (Clarity × Communication) ÷ Chaos

When clarity or communication drops, chaos fills the space. Project management is structure applied to work you already do.
— Intentional Outcomes, Ch. 1

Segment 1

Is It a Project?

Separate projects from operations and from time management. Commit to one Anchor Project.

METHOD STEP: SHELF CHECK
Definitions

Project, operation, or time management?

Project

A temporary effort that creates something new or better. Has a beginning and an end.

Migrating payroll systems

Operation

Ongoing, repeating work that keeps the organization running.

Running payroll

Time management

How you spend your hours. Project management is how a piece of work gets from idea to done, often through other people.

Often misdiagnosedA lot of what feels like a time management problem is really three unbounded projects pretending to be operations.
The Quick Test

Can we say when it will be finished?
Can we say what success looks like?

Two yeses means you manage it as a project. Anything else is an idea, a wish, or a problem. That's fine, but it goes on the Idea Log, not the calendar. (Intentional Outcomes, Ch. 1)

Group Exercise

Project or Operation?

Call it: Project, Operation, or Not Yet a Project (fails the Quick Test). We'll argue about the hard ones.

  • Weekly storytime

  • Migrating to a new ILS

  • Applying for NYS Construction Aid for a roof
  • "Improve our social media"
  • Moving the academic library's LibGuides to a new platform
  • "Be more welcoming to teens"
The Hard Part

Projects create operations.

  • The website redesign ends. The website doesn't. Who updates it, renews the hosting, and pays for it in year two?
  • Annual weeding is an operation. A one-time weeding to prepare for a move is a project.

The Method Map

Last Page First

"The book is called Intentional Outcomes for a reason: you decide the ending before you start."

The Method

Five Steps

StepQuestionYou walk away withGate: who has to say go?
0. Shelf CheckIs this a project?A yes or no, and an editionYou
1. Last PageWhat does the ending look like, and who can approve it?Purpose, success, done, out of scope, authoritySponsor approves the Last Page
2. Table of ContentsWhat are the chapters, and what will they cost in time and money?Deliverables, estimates, milestones, risks, change ruleSponsor agrees to the plan as the baseline
3. ChaptersHow do we write it one stage at a time?Board, weekly check, status reports, change logSponsor decides at chapter end: continue / adjust / stop
4. EpilogueHow do we finish on purpose?Verified result, owner, records, lessons, thank-yousSponsor accepts in writing
What's a gate?A gate is a checkpoint between steps. Before you move on, the person named in the last column has to say "go." If they say no, you fix the work first, before spending more time or money.
Why This Order?

Every later decision gets easier when the last page already exists.

  • Is this change in scope? Check the Last Page.
  • Is it done? Check the Last Page.
  • Who decides? Check the Last Page.
If you know PMIThese are the five process groups in library language: Initiating → Last Page; Planning → Table of Contents; Executing and Monitoring & Controlling → Chapters; Closing → Epilogue. A few tools are borrowed from agile, lean, and PRINCE2 where they fit small teams better.

Pamphlet, Paperback, or Hardcover?

0–3

Pamphlet

One page total. Email the sponsor for approval. A new storytime series.

4–7

Paperback

A one-page Last Page, deliverables with milestones, top-5 risks, monthly status, a written change rule, a closing checklist. A Library of Things launch.

8–10

Hardcover

The full book: charter, plan, WBS, risk register, communication plan, change log, quality checks, transition plan, audit-ready archive. Construction Aid, an ILS migration.

Write the Last Page

Turn the Anchor Project into a decision, before anyone commits time or money.

METHOD STEP: LAST PAGE (INITIATING)
Write the Ending First

If you can't describe the ending, you can't plan the middle.

Nobody's board cares that the ILS migrated. They care that patrons can find and request items without calling the desk.

Outcome vs. Output

The output is the thing. The outcome is what changes for people.

Output: the thing you make

A collection of 120 items on the shelf. You can count it and point at it.

Outcome: how people feel or what happens

Patrons save money and try new things. You see it in what they do and say.

  • Purpose answers Why now? and What will success look like?
  • Success: a number, a date, or an observable change. Put the outcome first, then the output.
  • Definition of Done: the conditions that must all be true to call it finished. (From Scrum. The same idea as PMI's acceptance criteria.)
  • Ask "so what?" after every deliverable until you reach a person who is better off.
The Authority Question

Who can say yes?
Who can say no?
Who can say "also"?

The third person is where scope creep comes from. Name all three on your Last Page.

Discussion

Who is the "also" person in your organization?

Name roles, not people. Common answers: a trustee, a funder, the director, a vocal patron, the city.

Library typeSponsor is often...
PublicThe board
AcademicThe dean or Associate University Librarian (AUL)
SchoolThe principal, and sometimes the district
Where public dollars are involvedThe sponsor's approval must be on official record: minutes or email.

One authorized project manager. One sponsor who controls resources. "Without that clarity, accountability dissolves."

SOLO · QUIET
Exercise A

Draft your Last Page

  1. Purpose: To [change] for [whom] by [when].
  2. Success looks like: 1-3 results (number, date, or something observable). Outcome first.
  3. Done means: 3-5 conditions, including "handed off to [owner]."
  4. Out of scope: at least two things you are not doing.
  5. Sponsor and PM: names, not committees.
  6. Who can say yes / no / "also"?

One or two lines each. No full sentences needed. Write it as if the project is already finished.

PAIRS
Exercise B

The Stranger Test

  1. Swap papers. The writer says nothing.
  2. The reader reads the page once, then turns it over.
  3. From memory, the reader answers:
    • What is this project trying to accomplish?
    • How will we know it's done?
    • Who is responsible for making it happen?
  4. The writer listens and takes notes. Switch. Then fix whatever your partner got wrong.

If they got it wrong, the problem is the page, not the reader.

Segment 3

Table of Contents

Break the work into deliverables, pick the smallest version that works, and protect the scope.

METHOD STEP: PLANNING (SCOPE AND WBS)
Work Breakdown Structure

List the chapters, not the to-dos.

  • Start from the finished product and break it into parts. "A WBS describes work, not people."
  • Deliverables are nouns. Work is verb + noun. "Signage" → "Draft signage text," "Get director approval," "Print and install."
  • Stop breaking down when a piece is small enough that one person could finish it in a week or two and you could estimate it. PMI calls that a work package.
  • It doesn't list who writes them, and it isn't a to-do list yet.
The First Edition

Launch the first edition.
Then plan the second.

Libraries tend to want to launch the perfect thing. Ask: what is the smallest version that still meets the Definition of Done? Everything else goes on the Later Editions list. (Lean's minimum viable product; the "Go" box on the Scope Evaluation Matrix.)

SOLO · QUIET
Exercise A

Sticky-note WBS

  1. For each Definition of Done condition, write the deliverable (a noun) on a sticky note.
  2. Under each, add 2-4 notes for the work (verb + noun).
  3. When the notes are down, star the First Edition: the smallest set that still means "done."
  4. Stick it all on your Table of Contents panel.
Know the Difference

Four ways scope changes

Scope creep

Intentional additions to scope, usually from someone with influence.

Scope drift

Small, unrecorded changes that pile up until the project is a different size.

Gold plating

The team adds extras nobody asked for.

A missed requirement

Not creep. The plan left out something required (accessibility, a code or licensing rule). It still goes through the change rule, but the answer is rarely "no."

PMI's top causes of scope creep: ambiguous scope, no formal requirements process, inconsistent requirements collection, weak sponsorship, long project duration.

Scope Evaluation Matrix

Importance vs. difficulty

NegotiateImportant, but hard. Trade it against scope, schedule, or budget.
GoImportant and easy. Fits the First Edition.
No-GoNot important, and hard. Decline, and write down why.
Negotiate / Later EditionEasy but not important. Park it on the Later Editions list.
Top of the grid: more important. Right side: easier. For each request, ask: Go, Negotiate, or No-Go? What kind of change is it? What do I say, and what do I write down?

The Rule

Every change is a trade.
"Yes, and here's what it costs" is a complete answer.

The Most Important Slide in This Section

Put it in writing.

  • A hallway yes isn't a decision. It's an assumption.
  • Every decision about scope, schedule, or money gets a written line: what, who, when, and the cost.
  • Everything involving money goes in the project file.
  • Construction Aid projects (and most grant or public-money projects) can be audited. If it isn't in the file, it didn't happen, or it wasn't approved.
This method plans the projectIt does not replace proper project documentation. Charter, change log, approvals, and invoices all belong in the project file.

Time and Money

Turn the Table of Contents into a realistic schedule and budget range. See how scope, time, and money trade against each other.

METHOD STEP: PLANNING (ESTIMATE)
The Triple Constraint

Pull one corner and the others move.

Scope

What you're delivering.

Schedule

When it's delivered.

Budget

What it costs.

With quality in the middle. If someone moves the deadline up, something else gives: scope shrinks, or you add money or people.

"But my project doesn't cost money."Everything costs money. If your project is staff time, the budget is hours. Forty hours of a librarian's time is real money even if it never shows up on an invoice. Count hours and put a dollar figure next to them (hours × loaded hourly rate).
Quality vs. Grade

Cutting grade is fine. Cutting quality isn't.

  • Quality: the deliverable does what you need it to (conformance to requirements, fitness for use).
  • Grade: how much people value it, its features and finish.
  • A simpler version can still be high quality.
  • When money or time gets squeezed, protect the outcome.
Estimating Methods

Three ways to get to a number

Analogous

"Last time it cost..."

$3,000 for 1,000 photos

Parametric

Cost per unit × units.

$3 per photo × 10,000

Bottom-up

Add up the work packages.

Most accurate, most effort

"Estimates aren't guesses. They're informed predictions with the assumptions written down."
PERT

(Optimistic + 4 × Most Likely + Pessimistic) ÷ 6

Worked example

6 weeks best, 8 most likely, 12 worst:

(6 + 4×8 + 12) ÷ 6 = 8.33

Plan for 9 weeks.

Rules

  • Works for time, cost, or hours
  • Always round up
  • Write down the basis and assumptions
  • Use on the 3 biggest or most uncertain work packages

Also: dependencies (what can't start until something else finishes) and milestones, not every task: "Identify the few milestones that will define progress."

Library Reality

Check your calendar.

  • Your biggest schedule risks are usually outside your control: board meeting dates, grant windows, application deadlines, the fiscal year, vendor lead times, summer.
  • Many projects need board approval at some point. Make each board meeting a milestone: what are you bringing, and what do you need decided?
  • Not a director? Same idea with your supervisor's meetings, one-on-ones, or your department head's calendar.
SOLO
Exercise A

Estimate your three biggest pieces

  1. Pick your 3 biggest or most uncertain work packages.
  2. Write Best / Likely / Worst for time (and cost, or hours).
  3. (Best + 4 × Likely + Worst) ÷ 6. Round up.
  4. Circle what can't start until something else is done.
  5. Draw a line across the bottom of your panel: 3-5 milestones plus your board or supervisor meeting dates.
GROUP
Exercise B

The Squeeze

Something just got squeezed by 20%: your budget, your hours, or your deadline. Talk it through as a group:

  1. What gives: scope, schedule, or budget?
  2. What does that do to the outcome?
  3. Would you make the same trade on your own project?

Protect the outcome. Cut grade before quality. Tell the sponsor what you chose.

The Pre-Mortem (Gary Klein, HBR 2007)

"What could go wrong?" gets polite answers. "It failed. Why?" gets honest ones.

  • Assume the project has already failed and explain why. Research on prospective hindsight finds it surfaces more risks than asking what might go wrong.
  • Keep it grounded. Think ordinary failures: a person leaves, a vendor is late, a decision stalls, the money's not what you thought.
  • A pandemic, a market crash, an AI takeover? That's disaster planning, a different conversation. Nobody expects you to finish a project when something far outside average possibility happens.
SOLO, THEN GROUP
Exercise B

Pre-mortem

It's a year from now. Your project failed. Not a disaster, but it clearly failed. Why?

  1. Write every reason you can think of, one per line (solo).
  2. Go around the room. One reason each, again and again, until the ideas run out. Every similar reason gets a star.
  3. Read the pattern: how many different projects, how many of the same failures?
  4. Solo: turn your top 3 into risks. If ___ happens, then ___. Response: Accept / Avoid / Transfer / Mitigate / Exploit. Put an owner's name next to each.
What the Stars Tell Us

Your project's details are unique.
Its failure modes aren't.

Sponsor lost interest or left · key person left · board added scope · vendor was late · nobody used the new thing · the grant report was a scramble. Things do go sideways. But if most of your projects fail, the one constant is how you plan them. That's fixable, and someone has already solved most of these problems. (Research on "uniqueness bias," Flyvbjerg et al., finds perceived uniqueness doesn't track actual uniqueness.)

Five ways to respond to a risk

AvoidChange the plan so the risk can't happenPut the policy on the March agenda so April isn't the deadline

Issue vs. risk: an issue has already happened; a risk might.

Segment 6

Write in Chapters

Run the work in stages, keep it visible, report status honestly, and handle change without micromanaging.

METHOD STEP: EXECUTING, MONITORING AND CONTROLLING
Manage by Stages (PRINCE2)

Chapters of 2-8 weeks, each ending in a milestone.

  • Chapter gate: a checkpoint at the end of each chapter. The sponsor looks at what's finished, what it cost, and what's next, then decides: continue, adjust, or stop.
  • Two habits carry most of the weight: a board everyone can see and a 15-minute weekly check.
  • Quality checks belong at each chapter gate, not only at the end.
Visualize Work, Limit Work in Progress

If you have seven projects in "Doing," you have seven projects in "Waiting."

To Do

Chapter work, ready to start.

Doing

No more than about 3 cards per person. This is your WIP (work in progress) limit.

Done

Finished and verified.

The board can be a whiteboard, sticky notes, Trello, Planner, or a spreadsheet. The tool doesn't matter. Keeping it current does. Finish before you start.

The Rhythm

Weekly questions. Four-part reports.

Weekly: three questions

  1. Are we where we expected to be?
  2. Have we spent more or less than planned?
  3. At this pace, will we finish on time and on budget?

Status report: four parts

  1. Status: on track, at risk, or off track?
  2. Achievements: what got finished since the last report
  3. Issues and risks
  4. Next steps and decisions needed
Boards need progress against milestones, budget, risks, and a decision. They don't need your task list.
Change and Oversight

Monitoring without micromanaging.

  • Integrated change control: identify, evaluate, decide, implement, report. Log every request and every decision, in writing, and file it.
  • Micromanagement is what happens when a supervisor can't see progress. Make progress visible and nobody has to hover.
  • "Oversight should feel like support, not interference." Watch the board and the milestones, not the people.
  • Portfolio check: continue, pause, or conclude. "Ending a project is not failure; it is stewardship."
SOLO
Exercise A

Build your chapters and board

  1. Group your work into 2-4 chapters, each ending at one of your milestones.
  2. Draw three columns: To Do | Doing | Done. Move Chapter 1 notes into To Do.
  3. Write your WIP (work in progress) limit at the top: the most things you can have in Doing at once.
  4. Running several projects? List them in the corner. Mark each Continue, Pause, or End. You're allowed to use E.
Exercise B · Part 1: pick one (or use a real one)

Something just happened...

  • Your key volunteer or staff member just resigned.
  • The vendor quote came in 30% over budget.
  • The board president wants it done a month early for a ribbon-cutting.
  • The grant funder changed its reporting requirements.
  • A trustee is telling the community about a feature that's out of scope.
  • You're a month behind because of a building closure.
  • A second funder wants to add money, with conditions.
  • Your sponsor is going on a three-month leave.
  • The municipality or district hasn't paid the invoice it promised.

  • The one person who knows the old system retires in six weeks.

  • The board approved the project, but the minutes don't mention the budget.
  • The contractor is asking for a change order.
  • A new director starts and wants to "take a fresh look" at the plan.
PAIRS
Exercise B · Part 2

Plot Twist and the Status Report

Take the plot twist you picked and report it to "the board" the way a project manager would.

  1. Sort it. An issue already happened (use your planned risk response). A change request is someone wanting something different (use your change rule).
  2. Write four lines on scrap paper, making up any details you need:
    • Status: on track, at risk, or off track?
    • Achievements: what has been finished so far
    • Issues and risks: the plot twist, and your response
    • Decision needed: what you need the board to decide
  3. Turn to a neighbor. Read your four lines out loud. They play the board and ask one question. Then switch.
Capture on Your Chapters Panel

What goes on the panel

  • 2-4 chapters, each with an end milestone and a chapter gate (Sponsor decides continue, adjust, or stop on ___)
  • Board: To Do / Doing / Done, with a WIP (work in progress) limit
  • Weekly check: which day, how long, the three questions
  • Status rhythm: who gets the four-part update and when (weekly for the team, monthly or per board meeting for the board)
  • Change rule and where requests get logged
  • Optional: your portfolio, each project marked Continue, Pause, or End

Segment 7

The Epilogue

Make closing a deliberate step: verify, hand off, learn, thank people, and archive.

METHOD STEP: CLOSING
Transition to Operations

Stewardship, not possession.

Someone has to own it on Monday. If a new service has no owner after launch, it will slowly be forgotten. "Completion is not the same as closure."

Closing Well

Go back to your Last Page.

  • Closing means checking every line of your Definition of Done. If you can't show it's done, it isn't done.
  • Say thank you on the record. Recognition is part of accountability.
  • Close the file. If it's grant or aid money, the project file is your audit defense.
  • "An unrecorded lesson is a mistake repeated."
That "still not quite done" project from the start of the day? This is the fix.
SOLO
Exercise A

Write the closing announcement

It's the day your project closes. Write the short announcement to your board, staff, or campus. It's the back-cover blurb of your book.

  • What was delivered
  • The result against your success measures
  • Who owns it now
  • Who you're thanking
  • Where the records live

Exercise B

Lessons learned: the ones you never wrote down

Think of a project you've already rolled out, finished or not. Talk it through as a group:

  • What did you learn during rollout that you would do again?
  • What did you learn that you would never do again?
  • Which of those lessons did you never write down?

This room already holds a lessons-learned list nobody has written down. Your project's review should happen, and be written down.

Capture on Your Epilogue Panel

What goes on the panel

  • Closing checks: each Definition of Done condition, and how you'll show it's met
  • New owner after handoff, and what they need (training, documentation, maintenance budget)
  • Money: final invoices, grant or aid reporting, leftover funds (reallocate only with approval)
  • Lessons: when you'll hold the review, and who's invited
  • Thank-yous: who gets thanked, and how
  • Archive: where the records live, e.g. 01_Charter, 02_Schedule and Budget, 03_Reports, 04_Meeting Notes and Approvals

Take-Home Checklist · 1 of 2

Last Page First: the method on one page

0. Shelf Check
Is this a project?

  • Can I say when it will be finished?
  • Can I say what success looks like?
  • What edition is it?

1. Last Page
Write the ending first.

  • Why this, why now, for whom?
  • What 1-3 measurable results mean success?
  • What must be true to call it done?
  • What are we not doing?
  • Who sponsors, who runs, who says yes / no / "also"?

Gate 1: sponsor approved, on the record?

2. Table of Contents
Plan the chapters.

  • Deliverables and work? First Edition and Later Editions?
  • Biggest pieces in best / likely / worst (PERT)?
  • 3-5 milestones vs. board, grant, fiscal dates?
  • Budget range, funding source, what gives first?
  • Who hears what, how often?
  • It failed: why?
  • How are changes requested and decided, in writing?

Gate 2: sponsor agreed to the plan as the baseline?

Take-Home Checklist · 2 of 2

Chapters and Epilogue

3. Chapters
Write it one chapter at a time.

  • Board visible and current? Within my WIP limit?
  • Weekly: on schedule? On budget? On pace to finish?
  • Status: status, achievements, issues and risks, decisions needed
  • Issue (respond) or change (log it and decide)?

Chapter gates: continue, adjust, or stop? Re-score the edition.

4. Epilogue
Finish on purpose.

  • Every Definition of Done condition verified and accepted in writing?
  • Who owns it now, and what do they need?
  • Invoices, reports, and leftover funds settled?
  • What worked, what didn't, what would we change? (written)
  • Everyone thanked and released? Records archived? Announcement sent?
